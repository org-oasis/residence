<p>The <strong>culinary specialties to try in Skikda</strong> fall into three families: seafood cuisine (fish and shellfish), the great Algerian classics (chorba, couscous, chakhchoukha, mhadjeb), and oriental pastries (baklawa, makroud). The province, 20 km from Filfila, combines an active coastline with a national culinary heritage that make it a complete food destination.</p>

<p>For a visitor discovering the region, Skikda's cuisine isn't limited to grilled fish alone, even if that's often the first image that comes to mind for a port city. This article covers the families of dishes worth knowing before you go, the best occasions to try them, and how to plan your stay to make the most of the local table, alongside a beach stay in Filfila.</p>

<h2>Seafood cuisine, the province's signature</h2>

<p>Skikda owes much of its culinary reputation to its coastline. The province's active fishing ports, starting with <a href="/en/blog/poisson-frais-stora">Stora and its fresh fish</a>, guarantee a daily supply that feeds directly into restaurants along the coast.</p>

<h3>Grilled fish and shellfish</h3>

<p>Grilled fish is the most common preparation: sardine, red mullet, sea bream or grouper depending on the catch, served simply with lemon and olive oil. Shellfish (squid, shrimp, octopus) round out the offering, particularly at restaurants set up along the port.</p>

<p>Beyond fish, Skikda shares the staples of Algerian cuisine, prepared according to family recipes passed down through generations.</p>

<h3>Chorba and couscous, the family-meal staples</h3>

<p>Chorba frik, a spiced broth made with cracked wheat and meat, traditionally opens the evening meal. Couscous, often reserved for Friday, remains the ultimate sharing dish, usually served with seasonal vegetables and meat or chicken.</p>

<h3>Mhadjeb, the popular snack</h3>

<p>Mhadjeb, a semolina flatbread stuffed with spiced tomato sauce, is easy to find as a street snack or a hearty breakfast in most towns across the province. It's a practical option for a quick meal between visits.</p>

<h2>Oriental pastries not to miss</h2>

<p>Pastry holds an important place in the local food culture, particularly during festive meals or at the end of the day with mint tea or coffee.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Baklawa</strong>: filo pastry, dried fruit and honey, in small portions</li>
  <li><strong>Makroud</strong>: semolina filled with dates, fried or baked</li>
  <li><strong>Kalb el louz</strong> ("almond heart"): semolina soaked in syrup and topped with almonds</li>
  <li><strong>Griouech</strong>: fried pastry shaped like a rosette, coated in honey</li>
</ul>

<h2>A cuisine at the crossroads of several influences</h2>

<p>Skikda's table reflects the province's position, between the Mediterranean and the inland regions. Seafood takes centre stage along the coast, while semolina and wheat dishes (couscous, chorba frik, chakhchoukha) reflect a broader place within inland Algerian cuisine. This dual influence makes it easy to vary meals throughout a stay, without sticking to a single family of dishes, whether you stay by the sea in Filfila or venture inland within the province.</p>

<h2>Traditional drinks that go with the meal</h2>

<p>An Algerian meal rarely ends without a hot drink or a fresh juice, an integral part of the culinary ritual in Skikda as elsewhere in the country.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Mint tea</strong>: served well sweetened, at the end of a meal or during visits</li>
  <li><strong>Algerian-style coffee</strong>: a strong espresso, often served with a glass of water</li>
  <li><strong>Fresh fruit juice</strong>: orange, lemon or seasonal fruit, very common in summer</li>
  <li><strong>Homemade lemonade</strong>: available in some seafront cafés</li>
</ul>

<h2>Common mistakes to avoid on the food side</h2>

<p>A few simple habits help avoid the most common letdowns for visitors discovering Skikda's cuisine for the first time.</p>

<ul>
  <li>Sticking to grilled fish alone without trying traditional dishes like chakhchoukha or couscous</li>
  <li>Arriving late at a restaurant expecting the same fish selection as at lunch</li>
  <li>Overlooking oriental pastries, often served in small portions to share rather than as an individual dessert</li>
  <li>Underestimating the generous size of traditional portions, especially for Friday couscous</li>
</ul>

<h2>When and where to try these specialties</h2>

<p>The summer months, from June to November for the swimming season, bring the widest choice of restaurants and pastry shops, driven by tourist footfall. Stora's port remains the reference for fresh fish, while central Skikda and addresses in Filfila cover more of the traditional dishes and neighbourhood pastries.</p>

<h3>A week is enough for a full overview</h3>

<p>A stay of about a week is enough to cover the essentials: a few fish meals in Stora, a couscous or chakhchoukha at a traditional restaurant, a mhadjeb as a quick snack, and an oriental pastry to close out each day. <h3>A family-friendly discovery</h3>

<p>Most of these specialties work well for a family meal, with generous portions and dishes designed to be shared, such as couscous or chakhchoukha served in the middle of the table. Oriental pastries, presented as small bites, also let children try several textures without committing to a full portion. That's worth keeping in mind when planning meals during a family stay without multiplying individual orders.</p>
