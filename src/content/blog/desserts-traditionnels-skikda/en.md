<p>The <strong>traditional desserts to try in Skikda</strong> come down to four main families: baklawa, makroud, kalb el louz and griouech, all based on semolina, honey and dried fruit. These pastries are found mainly in central Skikda, about 20 km from Filfila, and traditionally accompany big family meals and religious occasions across the country.</p>

<p>For a visitor discovering the region, these sweets often get less attention than grilled fish or couscous, even though they hold just as central a place in the local food culture. This article covers the desserts worth knowing before your trip, where to find them around Filfila, the occasions they're traditionally tied to, and how to make them yourself if your accommodation has an equipped kitchen.</p>

<h2>The four essential traditional desserts</h2>

<p>These pastries are found throughout Algeria, but remain particularly present in Skikda during family meals and big occasions. Each has its own texture and preparation method, which makes it easy to vary the pleasure over the course of a stay.</p>

<h3>Baklawa, the star of oriental pastries</h3>

<p>Baklawa is made of thin layers of filo pastry, filled with chopped dried fruit (almonds, walnuts, pistachios) and soaked in honey syrup. Served in small squares or diamonds, it appears on almost every table during family occasions and throughout the summer months, when tourist demand adds to local consumption.</p>

<h3>Makroud, semolina filled with dates</h3>

<p>Makroud is made from a fine semolina dough, filled with a date paste, then fried or baked before being dipped in honey or syrup. Its texture, somewhere between soft and crisp depending on the cooking method, makes it one of the most popular desserts to pair with an Algerian coffee at the end of a meal.</p>

<h3>Kalb el louz, "heart of almond"</h3>

<p>Kalb el louz, literally "heart of almond," is a semolina cake soaked in syrup flavoured with orange blossom water and topped with almonds. Its moist, soft texture makes it easy to share in small portions, enjoyed by children and adults alike.</p>

<h3>Griouech, the crisp honey rosette</h3>

<p>Griouech is a thin pastry cut into a rosette shape, fried and then coated in honey. Crisp and light despite its sweet coating, it works well in small quantities, paired with a mint tea.</p>

<h2>Other sweets worth trying</h2>

<p>Beyond the four classics, a few other preparations are worth trying during a stay in Skikda, especially for big family or religious occasions.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Cornes de gazelle (kaab el ghazal)</strong>: thin crescent-shaped pastry filled with almond paste flavoured with orange blossom water</li>
  <li><strong>Zlabia</strong>: fried pastry in a spiral shape, dipped in honey syrup, very common during Ramadan</li>
  <li><strong>Tamina</strong>: toasted semolina mixed with honey and butter, a simple dessert prepared at home</li>
  <li><strong>Basboussa</strong>: semolina cake soaked in syrup, sometimes topped with whole almonds</li>
</ul>

<h3>Central Skikda, the reference for variety</h3>

<p>Central Skikda has the largest number of traditional pastry shops, with a wider choice of baklawa, makroud and cornes de gazelle than in Filfila. It's the most reliable place to try several varieties in a single outing, ideally before heading back to the coast.</p>

<h3>Filfila, convenient for everyday needs</h3>

<p>In Filfila itself, local shops in the Jeanne d'Arc neighbourhood make it easy to satisfy a small sweet craving without a trip to the city centre. The choice there stays more limited than at a specialised pastry shop, though.</p>

<h2>Desserts tied to big occasions</h2>

<p>A significant share of these pastries isn't eaten every day, but reserved for specific moments in the family and religious calendar, which explains their festive character and often collective preparation.</p>

<h3>Weddings and family gatherings</h3>

<p>Baklawa, makroud and cornes de gazelle take centre stage at weddings and big family gatherings, where they're often prepared in large quantities several days ahead and presented on large platters.</p>

<h3>Ramadan and breaking the fast</h3>

<p>During Ramadan, zlabia and kalb el louz traditionally accompany the breaking of the fast, alongside savoury dishes like chorba. It's a period when demand for traditional pastry rises sharply across the whole country, Skikda included.</p>

<h2>Can you make these desserts yourself in an apartment?</h2>

<p>For travellers who enjoy cooking on holiday, several of these desserts remain accessible with simple equipment: an oven, a hob and a few basic utensils. <strong>Tamina</strong>, for example, needs only a pan and a bit of patience, no oven required. A simplified <strong>kalb el louz</strong> can also be made without specialised equipment.</p>

It's a popular activity for families, especially with children curious to help with the preparation.</p>

<h2>Common mistakes to avoid</h2>

<p>A few simple habits help avoid the most common letdowns for visitors trying these pastries for the first time.</p>

<ul>
  <li>Ordering a full individual portion expecting a Western-style dessert: these pastries are eaten in small bites, often shared</li>
  <li>Underestimating how rich in honey and dried fruit some pastries are, especially baklawa and makroud</li>
  <li>Sticking to Filfila's pastries without making the trip to central Skikda for a wider choice</li>
  <li>Showing up late in the day during peak summer season at the most popular pastry shops, risking a reduced selection</li>
  <li>Overlooking storage: these honey-based desserts keep better at room temperature in a closed box than in the fridge</li>
</ul>

<h2>Pairing desserts with traditional drinks</h2>

<p>These pastries are almost always paired with a hot drink, an integral part of the tasting ritual. <strong>Mint tea</strong>, served well sweetened, remains the most classic pairing, while <strong>Algerian-style coffee</strong>, stronger and more bitter, works especially well with makroud or baklawa at the end of a meal.
