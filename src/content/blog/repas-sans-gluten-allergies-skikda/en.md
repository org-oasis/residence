<p>Traditional Algerian cuisine relies heavily on wheat, as semolina for couscous or flour for bread and pastries, which complicates life for gluten-intolerant travelers. On top of that come seafood allergies, common in a coastal region where fish holds a central place, or tree-nut allergies found in some local sweets. This article covers which dishes to favor or avoid, how to communicate an allergy at a restaurant, and why cooking for yourself in the apartment is often the safest solution during a stay in Filfila.</p>

<h2>Gluten in traditional Algerian cuisine</h2>

<p>Wheat holds a central place in the Algerian diet, which makes gluten intolerance particularly tricky to manage while traveling without knowing which dishes are affected.</p>

<h3>Naturally gluten-free dishes</h3>

<p>Several local specialties contain no gluten in their original preparation, provided you check for breading or a flour-based sauce:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Grilled meats (merguez without a flour-based casing, skewers, grilled chicken)</li>
  <li>Grilled fish, widely available on the Skikda coast, plain or marinated with spices</li>
  <li>Raw or cooked vegetable salads without a flour-thickened sauce</li>
  <li>Fresh fruit and plain dairy products</li>
  <li>Rice and some meat or vegetable tagines simmered without a flour thickener</li>
</ul>

<h3>Dishes to avoid or systematically check</h3>

<p>Conversely, several staples of local cuisine almost always contain gluten:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Couscous, made from wheat semolina</li>
  <li>Traditional bread (kesra, matlou3, flatbread) served with almost every meal</li>
  <li>Oriental pastries, often made with flour and layered dough</li>
  <li>Breaded fried dishes (breaded fish, breaded meats)</li>
  <li>Some sauces and broths thickened with flour</li>
</ul>

<h2>Eating out in Skikda with an allergy or intolerance</h2>

<p>In most restaurants across the province, a certified "gluten-free" menu remains uncommon. That does not make eating safely impossible, but it does mean direct communication with staff becomes essential with every order.</p>

<h3>How to phrase your request clearly</h3>

<p>Stating the allergy or intolerance before ordering, rather than after choosing a dish, lets staff suggest an alternative or flag a possible cross-contamination risk (a shared cutting board, frying oil shared with breaded items). A simple, direct statement in French or Arabic is understood in most tourist-facing establishments in the region.</p>

<h3>The risk of cross-contamination</h3>

<p>Even a normally gluten-free dish (grilled fish, grilled meats) can face cross-contamination if the same grill or the same oil is also used for breaded dishes. For a severe allergy, it remains best to explicitly ask for separate preparation rather than assume no contact occurs.</p>

<h2>Checklist before leaving with an allergy or intolerance</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Prepare a written list of allergens to avoid, in French and if possible in Arabic</li>
  <li>Bring a basic stock of gluten-free products from home if local availability is uncertain for very specific items (bread, pasta)</li>
  <li>Book a place with an equipped kitchen to be able to prepare your own meals when in doubt</li>
  <li>Always state the allergy before ordering at a restaurant, never after the dish arrives</li>
  <li>Systematically ask about the composition of sauces and mixed dishes (meat and vegetables)</li>
</ul>
