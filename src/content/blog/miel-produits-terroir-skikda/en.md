<p><strong>Honey and local produce</strong> near Skikda are mainly found at the city-centre market, about 20 km from Filfila, and in a handful of specialty grocers and agricultural cooperatives across the wilaya. Expect multiflower or eucalyptus honey, olive oil, dried figs and artisanal jams. The small shops in Filfila mainly cover everyday needs; for a wider choice, Skikda city centre is the place to go.</p>

<p>For a traveller staying at Résidence Oasis, bringing home a few local products is one of the small pleasures that keep the holiday memory alive long after the return. Between mountain honey, locally pressed olive oil and dried fruit sold by weight at the stalls, the Skikda region offers a modest but genuine terroir, provided you know where to look and when to visit the markets for the freshest goods.</p>

<h2>Honey from the Skikda region</h2>

<p>Beekeeping remains a common activity in eastern Algeria, and the wilaya of Skikda is no exception thanks to its forested massifs and maquis areas that provide varied melliferous flora throughout the year. The resulting honey comes in several varieties depending on the harvest season and the plants the bees have foraged.</p>

<h3>The most common varieties</h3>

<p>On eastern Algerian markets, three main families of honey tend to appear: multiflower honey, from a varied flora with a milder taste; eucalyptus honey, stronger and slightly minty; and, more rarely, jujube honey, prized for its distinctive aroma. Market sellers rarely display strict certification, which makes tasting before buying worthwhile when offered, along with relying on local recommendations rather than labelling alone.</p>

<h3>Recognising quality honey</h3>

<p>A few simple cues help tell artisanal honey apart from an overly processed product: a texture that crystallises gradually over time (a sign of honey that has not been heavily heated), a clear smell matching the stated variety, and a seller who can describe the harvest area with precision. Honey that stays liquid indefinitely or tastes uniformly sweet without nuance is worth treating with caution.</p>

<h2>Olive oil and olive-tree products</h2>

<p>The wilaya of Skikda is hilly, with numerous olive groves on the heights overlooking the coast, from Collo to the edges of Filfila. This landscape supports a local olive-oil production, sold in cans or bottles at traditional markets, often directly from small producers or their intermediaries.</p>

<h3>The harvest season</h3>

<p>Olive harvesting generally runs from November to January in this part of Algeria. This is when the new, freshest and most aromatic oil reaches the stalls. Outside this window, the oil available comes from older stock, which remains usable but loses some of its aromatic character.</p>

<h3>Table olives and derivatives</h3>

<p>Beyond oil, table olives (green or black, brine-cured or dried) are also sold at markets, often loose and by weight. They go well with a simple meal prepared in the apartment's equipped kitchen, together with fresh bread bought the same morning.</p>

<h2>Dried figs, dried fruit and artisanal jams</h2>

<p>Dried figs are among the easiest terroir products to find and carry home. Sun-dried and sometimes stuffed with almond paste depending on the seller, they keep for a long time without refrigeration, a clear advantage for a tasty souvenir to bring back. For a fuller calendar of fresh seasonal fruit, the article on <a href="/en/blog/fruits-agrumes-filfila-skikda">local fruit and citrus to enjoy in Filfila</a> covers the best times of year.</p>

<h3>Almonds, walnuts and dates</h3>

<p>Dried fruit (almonds, walnuts, roasted peanuts) completes the stalls, sold by weight and often offered for tasting before purchase. Dates, although they mostly come from the country's Saharan regions rather than the coast, remain widely available on eastern Algerian markets year-round and make a classic souvenir.</p>

<h3>Artisanal jams and pastries</h3>

<p>Fig, quince or apricot jams can be found in some specialty grocers and at the markets, made by small local producers. They pair well with a plate of traditional pastries; the article on <a href="/en/blog/desserts-traditionnels-skikda">traditional desserts to try in Skikda</a> details the sweets worth tasting during the stay.</p>

<h3>Storage during the stay</h3>

<p>Honey keeps very well at room temperature, away from direct sunlight, with no need for refrigeration. Olive oil also keeps outside the fridge, in a closed container and out of the light. Only opened jams and certain fresh products benefit from being refrigerated once started.</p>

<h2>Bringing these products back home</h2>

<p>Honey and olive oil count as liquids for air travel and must go in checked luggage rather than hand luggage, beyond the small formats airlines allow. Since no airport sits directly in Skikda, the flight home goes through either Constantine airport (Mohamed Boudiaf), about 100 km away, or Annaba airport (Rabah Bitat), about 110 km away. It is best to plan for these purchases in a checked suitcase from the start, well wrapped in clothing to avoid breaking glass containers.</p>

<h3>Reasonable quantities</h3>

<p>For a flight, two or three jars of honey and one bottle of olive oil remain reasonable quantities to carry without excess weight. For a group wanting to bring back more, splitting the purchases across several bags avoids exceeding usual weight allowances.</p>

<h2>Common mistakes to avoid</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Buying large quantities of honey or olive oil without tasting beforehand</li>
  <li>Waiting until the last day of the stay to look for these products, risking a closed market</li>
  <li>Carrying glass bottles unprotected in checked luggage</li>
  <li>Confusing new-harvest oil (November-January) with older stock sold at the same price</li>
  <li>Forgetting to ask the seller for the exact harvest area, a useful clue to judge freshness</li>
</ul>
