<p>Before booking a stay in Skikda, it helps to know Algeria's <strong>public holiday calendar</strong>: five fixed civil dates (January 1, Yennayer on January 12, May 1, July 5, November 1) and several moving religious festivals, including Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha, which shift roughly 10 to 11 days earlier each year on the Gregorian calendar. These periods concentrate family travel and can tighten availability along the coast.</p>

<p>For an English-speaking traveler or a diaspora family planning a stay in Filfila, in the wilaya of Skikda, ignoring this calendar risks two inconveniences: booking an apartment right in the middle of peak demand without anticipating it, or arriving on a day when some shops are running at reduced pace. This article details the civil and religious holidays worth knowing, their concrete impact on a stay, and the reflexes to adopt to book at the right time.</p>

<h2>Algeria's fixed civil holidays</h2>

<p>Five civil holidays punctuate the Algerian calendar on the same fixed date every year. They're useful to know for anticipating long weekends among travelers coming from within Algeria itself, particularly from Constantine or Annaba.</p>

<h3>Limited impact on accommodation availability</h3>

<p>These isolated civil holidays generally have a modest effect on demand for lodging in Filfila compared to the major religious festivals. July 5, in the middle of the summer season, can nonetheless coincide with a peak in attendance already driven by school holidays, without being the main cause.</p>

<h2>Moving religious festivals</h2>

<p>Unlike civil holidays, <strong>Muslim religious festivals</strong> follow the lunar Hijri calendar, which counts about 354 days against 365 for the Gregorian calendar. As a result, each festival moves roughly 10 to 11 days earlier from one year to the next on the civil calendar, and can gradually shift from one season to another over several years.</p>

<h3>Eid al-Fitr, the end of Ramadan</h3>

<p>Eid al-Fitr marks the end of the Ramadan fasting month. It's celebrated over two to three days with family visits, new clothes for children, and traditional pastries exchanged between neighbors. Internal family travel within Algeria is significant during this period, which can reduce availability in touristic areas like Filfila if Eid falls outside the low season.</p>

<h3>Eid al-Adha, the feast of sacrifice</h3>

<p>Eid al-Adha occurs about two months and ten days after Eid al-Fitr. It's organized around the ritual sacrifice and a large family meal. As with Eid al-Fitr, travel between regions increases noticeably in the days before and after the festival.</p>

<h3>Mawlid and the Islamic New Year</h3>

<p>Mawlid, celebrating the Prophet's birth, and the Islamic New Year (1st of Muharram) are quieter religious festivals in public life compared to the two Eids, without comparable family travel. They still count as official holidays with closures or reduced hours for some businesses.</p>

<h2>Why these dates shift every year</h2>

<p>The annual drift of religious festivals comes from the purely lunar nature of the Hijri calendar, with no intercalary month to resynchronize it with the seasons the way the Hebrew calendar does. The exact starting date of each festival also depends on crescent moon sighting, which can push the official announcement to the evening before. For a stay planned several months ahead, it's therefore more reliable to check an up-to-date Algerian calendar close to the travel date than to rely on an approximate date calculated in advance.</p>

<h2>Concrete impact on a stay in Filfila</h2>

<p>Beyond the customs themselves, these festivals have very real practical consequences for anyone planning a beach stay in the wilaya of Skikda.</p>

<h3>Accommodation availability</h3>

<p>When a religious festival falls during swimming season (June to November), the combination of school holidays and family travel tied to the festival can quickly fill up the most sought-after accommodations in Filfila. Booking several weeks ahead via WhatsApp, with a one-night deposit by CCP transfer, remains the safest way to secure dates in this context.</p>

<h3>Shop and restaurant hours</h3>

<p>On the day of an Eid itself, some shops and restaurants close or reduce their hours, particularly during the first two days of the celebration. Doing essential grocery shopping the day before a religious holiday avoids unpleasant surprises, especially for an apartment with an equipped kitchen where self-catering is planned.</p>

<h3>Atmosphere and road traffic</h3>

<p>In the days leading up to an Eid, traffic between major Algerian cities (including on routes toward Constantine or Annaba, the closest airport access points to Skikda) can be heavier than usual. Building in extra time for a transfer on the day of a festival is a simple precaution.</p>

<h2>Ramadan: a special period, neither closed nor identical to the rest of the year</h2>

<p>The month of Ramadan deserves separate treatment, since it's not an isolated holiday but an entire month with a different rhythm. Days are quieter, some shops open later and close in the late afternoon before reopening after the fast is broken, a moment when streets and cafés regain a particular liveliness late into the evening.</p>

<p>A non-Muslim visitor remains free to eat and drink in their accommodation during the day; discretion in public places is appreciated out of respect for people fasting nearby.
