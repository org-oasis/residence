<p><strong>Jellyfish and other sea hazards</strong> do exist at Filfila, but remain an occasional risk rather than a systematic threat. The swimming season runs from June to November, with water reaching around 26°C in August; it's precisely at the end of summer that isolated jellyfish are most often reported, without their presence being guaranteed from one year to the next. Sea urchins, meanwhile, are mostly found on the coast's rocky stretches rather than on fine sand.</p>

<p>For a traveler planning a family or friends' swim at Filfila, the question deserves an undramatic answer: what's the real risk in the water, and what simple steps help avoid a bad surprise or handle it calmly if it happens? This article details the main sea hazards in the region, how common they really are, the right responses to an incident, and the precautions that are largely enough to enjoy the sea without excessive worry.</p>

<h2>Jellyfish along the Skikda coast</h2>

<p>The <strong>presence of jellyfish</strong> along the Algerian Mediterranean coast follows a fairly classic seasonal logic: it stays rare early in summer, when the water is still cool, and becomes somewhat more likely late in the season, when water temperature peaks around August.</p>

<h3>An occasional presence, not a systematic one</h3>

<p>Unlike some western Mediterranean areas where jellyfish shoals return predictably each year, the Skikda coast doesn't experience this phenomenon on a regular basis. Some seasons pass without any notable sighting, others see a few isolated individuals appear, carried by currents depending on wind and the moment's weather.</p>

<h3>Reading the signs before entering the water</h3>

<p>A quick glance before swimming often helps anticipate things: locally cloudier water, a few jellyfish washed up on the sand, or a cluster of swimmers avoiding a specific spot are useful clues. At supervised beaches, staff present during the season generally flag any notable presence observed during the day.</p>

<h2>What to do for a jellyfish sting</h2>

<p>A <strong>jellyfish sting</strong> reaction stays, in the vast majority of cases, limited to local pain and brief redness, but a few simple steps significantly reduce the discomfort.</p>

<h3>Immediate right responses</h3>

<ul>
  <li>Rinse the affected area thoroughly with seawater, never fresh water, which can reactivate the stinging cells</li>
  <li>Gently remove any visible tentacle residue, without rubbing with bare hands or a towel</li>
  <li>Apply warm water or vinegar if available to ease the burning sensation</li>
  <li>Avoid scratching the area, even if the itching persists over the following hours</li>
</ul>

<h3>When to see a healthcare professional</h3>

<p>A limited local reaction generally doesn't require a consultation. An extensive reaction over a large part of the body, significant swelling, breathing difficulty, or general malaise, on the other hand, warrant seeing someone quickly. An <a href="/en/blog/pharmacie-garde-filfila">on-call pharmacy at Filfila</a> remains reachable for a first opinion, and national emergency numbers apply for more serious situations.</p>

<h2>Sea urchins on the rocky stretches</h2>

<p>Along the Skikda coast, the rocky areas that alternate with sandy beaches naturally shelter <strong>sea urchins</strong>, nestled in shallow crevices accessible on foot or during light snorkeling.</p>

<h3>Where the risk is highest</h3>

<p>The risk mainly concerns rocky stretches rather than the fine sand of Larbi Ben M'hidi beach, where family attendance largely dominates. Walking barefoot on submerged rocks or ones lightly covered in algae increases the risk of accidental contact with a sea urchin.</p>

<h3>Prevention over treatment</h3>

<p>Simple water shoes eliminate nearly all of this risk on rocky areas. Should a sting happen anyway, broken spines under the skin should be removed carefully, ideally by a professional if they're deep, to avoid a local infection.</p>

<h2>Currents and rough seas</h2>

<p>Beyond marine animals, the main danger is often the most classic one: <strong>localized currents</strong> that form in strong wind or rough seas, particularly outside supervised areas.</p>

<h2>Simple precautions for the whole family</h2>

<p>None of these precautions require special equipment or complex preparation; they mostly rely on common sense and reasonable vigilance, without turning swimming into a source of constant worry.</p>

<ul>
  <li>Favor supervised beaches during the swimming season, June to November</li>
  <li>Watch the water for a moment before entering, especially in late summer</li>
  <li>Bring lightweight water shoes for outings on rocky stretches</li>
  <li>Keep vinegar or a basic first-aid kit in the beach bag</li>
  <li>Watch children more closely in strong wind or rough sea conditions</li>
</ul>

<h2>Snorkeling and diving: the same precautions</h2>

<p>A snorkeling outing or a <a href="/en/blog/plongee-skikda">diving session at Skikda</a> logically brings you closer to the same marine actors, since it brings the swimmer nearer the rocky areas where sea urchins and small rockfish concentrate. The same reflexes apply: water shoes or fins to avoid any direct contact with the bottom, observing without handling the organisms encountered, and heightened caution as soon as swell makes the water murkier and visibility lower.</p>

<h3>No reason to avoid the area</h3>

<p>These precautions signal no particular risk zone: they're simply the basic rules that apply to any Mediterranean coast alternating sand and rocks. The vast majority of stays at Filfila go by without the slightest marine incident, jellyfish or sea urchin alike, across the entire swimming season from June to November.</p>
