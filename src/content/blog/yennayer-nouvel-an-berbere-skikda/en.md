<p><strong>Yennayer</strong>, the New Year of the Berber agrarian calendar, is celebrated every <strong>12 January</strong> across Algeria, including in Skikda province, where it has been a national public holiday since 2018. The celebration stays family-based and low-key: a festive meal, often poultry and dried fruit, rather than a public event in the streets of Filfila or the town centre.</p>

<p>For a traveller planning a stay in Skikda in January, Yennayer raises a simple question: what actually happens on that day, and is it worth building a trip around this date? This article covers Yennayer's origin, how it is lived in an Arabic-speaking province like Skikda, what a visitor can realistically observe, and how to enjoy an off-season stay in Filfila during this quiet time of year.</p>

<h2>What Yennayer is and why it falls on 12 January</h2>

<p>The name <strong>Yennayer</strong> derives directly from the Latin "januarius", a trace of the agrarian Julian calendar used in North Africa before the Gregorian calendar became widespread. This agricultural calendar was used to structure farming work, independently of the Muslim religious calendar, which follows a lunar cycle.</p>

<h3>An agrarian calendar, not a religious one</h3>

<p>Unlike Eid al-Fitr or Eid al-Adha, Yennayer has no religious origin. It marks the start of a new agricultural year and is celebrated by practising and non-practising families alike, across all regions of Algeria, whether Berber-speaking or Arabic-speaking.</p>

<h3>National public holiday since 2018</h3>

<p>The Algerian government made Yennayer an official <strong>national public holiday</strong> in 2018, extending a practice that until then had mainly been rooted in Berber-speaking regions (Kabylie, Aurès, M'zab). Since then, 12 January has been a holiday nationwide, including in Skikda province. To place Yennayer within the full calendar of Algerian public holidays, <a href="/en/blog/jours-feries-algerie-skikda">the calendar of Algerian public holidays</a> covers all the civil and religious dates worth knowing before booking a stay.</p>

<h2>How Yennayer is traditionally celebrated in Algeria</h2>

<p>The way <strong>Yennayer</strong> is celebrated varies from region to region, but shares a common base centred on the family meal and a few symbolic customs tied to abundance for the year ahead.</p>

<h3>The festive meal</h3>

<p>The main dish is most often poultry cooked with dried legumes and seasonal vegetables, a meal deliberately more generous than an everyday one. Dried fruits and nuts (dates, walnuts, almonds, dried figs) are traditionally served as dessert or a side, symbolizing prosperity for the coming agricultural year.</p>

<h3>Symbolic customs passed down within families</h3>

<p>In several regions, some families avoid lending money or objects on this particular day, out of a superstition tied to not "starting the year" in lack. Other customs, such as dressing children in new clothes, exist locally but vary considerably from one family and region to another: these are orally transmitted traditions, with no single official calendar.</p>

<h2>Yennayer in Skikda and the Filfila area</h2>

<p>Skikda province is mostly Arabic-speaking, unlike regions such as Kabylie where Yennayer holds a more visible cultural place in public life. Still, since its official recognition in 2018, Yennayer is observed in Skikda as it is across the rest of the country.</p>

<h3>A family celebration rather than a street event</h3>

<p>In Skikda as in Filfila, Yennayer does not bring public festivities comparable to those seen in some towns in Kabylie. The day is mostly lived indoors, around the festive meal. A passing visitor will mainly notice shops and administrative offices closed, as with any public holiday, rather than dedicated street animation.</p>

<h3>What a visitor can observe on site</h3>

<p>A stay in Skikda during the Yennayer period mainly allows observing the general atmosphere of an Algerian public holiday: a quieter pace, shops closed on 12 January, then a normal resumption the next day. To go further into local customs and celebrations beyond this single date, <a href="/en/blog/traditions-locales-skikda">the local traditions and celebrations to discover in Skikda</a> covers the province's full calendar, between religious heritage and neighbourhood life.</p>

<h2>Yennayer across Algeria's regions</h2>

<p>The intensity of the <strong>Yennayer</strong> celebration varies considerably across the country's regions. In Kabylie, the Aurès or the M'zab, where Berber-speaking identity remains very present day to day, Yennayer holds a visible cultural place: decorations, traditional dress, and sometimes public cultural events. In an Arabic-speaking province like Skikda, the celebration stays comparatively more low-key, lived mainly indoors, without this calling into question its status as a national public holiday shared across the country.</p>

<h3>A heritage older than the Arab and French presence</h3>

<p>This regional diversity is a reminder that the territory of present-day Skikda province itself carries several successive layers of settlement, from the ancient city of Rusicade to the colonial town of Philippeville. <a href="/en/blog/histoire-ville-skikda">The history of the city of Skikda</a> details this succession of settlements, of which Berber and Numidian heritage forms one of the oldest layers, well before the arrival of the Muslim calendar that Yennayer precisely precedes.</p>
